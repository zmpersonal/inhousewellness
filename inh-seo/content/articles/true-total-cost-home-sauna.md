<p>Running a home sauna costs less than almost everyone expects. Three 45-minute sessions a week in a 2.1 kW infrared cabin comes to about <strong>$45 a year</strong> at the US average electricity rate. An 8 kW traditional room is about <strong>$172</strong>.</p>

<p><strong>So if you came here to work out the running cost, the honest answer is that it is not your decision.</strong> The electrician and the delivery are. Getting the crate inside is $600, getting it assembled is $1,800, and a dedicated 240V circuit can cost more than either — once, at the start, before you have had a single session. A $1,400 difference in installation dwarfs a $130 difference in annual electricity, and it is not spread over ten years.</p>

<p>That argues against the premise of this page and it is still the most useful thing we can tell you. Here is every line, in the order the money actually leaves.</p>

<h2>1. The purchase price is not the price</h2>

<p>Freight to the lower 48 is free with no minimum, and it stops at the end of your driveway. A sauna crate is heavy and awkward, and that last thirty feet is a real job.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Curbside:</strong> free. The crate is left at the kerb and it is yours from there.</li>
<li><strong>Inside delivery:</strong> $600. Into the room, still crated.</li>
<li><strong>Inside and assembled:</strong> $1,800. This covers everything except electrical work.</li>
</ul>

<p>Two things worth knowing before you choose. <strong>Service fees are non-refundable</strong>: if the sauna goes back, the installation does not. And <strong>$1,800 is all-in except the wiring</strong>, which is not a caveat in the small print — it is the largest remaining cost and it belongs to a different trade.</p>

<h2>2. The electrical circuit, which is where the surprises live</h2>

<p>This is the biggest variable in the whole exercise and the one nobody quotes you up front, because it depends on your house rather than on the sauna.</p>

<p>Most infrared cabins run on a standard 120V circuit and need nothing more than a free outlet. Traditional rooms and the larger infrared builds generally need a dedicated 240V circuit, run from your panel by a licensed electrician and inspected to local code.</p>

<p>The distance between those two situations is not small. A short run to a panel in the same room is a different job from a trench across a garden to an outdoor cabin, or from a panel that has no spare slots and needs upgrading first.</p>

<p><strong>We are not going to publish a national average for this, because a national average would be a made-up number.</strong> What we can do is tell you exactly what to ask, so the quote you get is a real one:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>How far is the sauna from the panel</strong>, and does the run go through finished walls, an attic, or outside?</li>
<li><strong>Does the panel have two spare slots</strong> at the amperage the sauna needs? If not, the panel upgrade is its own project and its own permit.</li>
<li><strong>Indoor or a trench?</strong> An outdoor cabin needs buried conduit and a weatherproof disconnect, and the digging is usually the expensive part.</li>
</ul>

<p>Take the sauna's spec sheet to the electrician before you order, not after. If the answer changes which sauna you buy, you want to know while you can still change it.</p>

<h2>3. What it costs to run, in full</h2>

<p>Running cost is arithmetic and there is no mystery in it:</p>

<p><strong>kilowatts × hours × sessions per week × your state's rate.</strong></p>

<p>Your rate is the part that varies most. Across the states the US residential average is about 18 cents a kilowatt-hour, and the spread runs from around 13 cents in Nevada to over 52 cents in Hawaii. Where you live can triple the answer.</p>

<p>Three 45-minute sessions a week, every week of the year:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>A 2.1 kW infrared cabin:</strong> about $32 a year in the cheapest states, $45 at the US average, $130 in Hawaii.</li>
<li><strong>An 8 kW traditional room:</strong> about $123 a year in the cheapest states, $172 at the US average, $493 in Hawaii.</li>
</ul>

<p>Even the worst case here — a big traditional room in the most expensive state in the country — is under $500 a year. That is the number to hold against the install quote when you are deciding.</p>

<h2>4. The number most manufacturers will not give you</h2>

<p>All of the above depends on knowing the kilowatt rating, and <strong>fewer than a third of the saunas we sell publish one.</strong> 151 of 481 active products state a kW figure anywhere in their listing. The rest do not.</p>

<p>That is not a fact about our catalogue. It is a fact about the category, and it has a consequence worth stating plainly: <strong>if the manufacturer has not published a rating, nobody selling that unit can tell you what it costs to run.</strong> That includes us. Any retailer quoting you a running cost for an unrated unit has estimated it.</p>

<p><strong>Ask for the kilowatt rating before you buy.</strong> A manufacturer who will not state it is telling you something. If we do not have it for a unit you are looking at, we will say so rather than guess.</p>

<h2>5. Five years, which is the framing that changes the decision</h2>

<p>Add it up over five years and the ranking can invert.</p>

<p>A cheaper cabin that needs a 240V circuit and a panel upgrade can cost more in year one than a dearer cabin that plugs into an outlet you already have — and then the two run within a hundred dollars a year of each other for the next four. The purchase price is the number on the page. It is rarely the number that decides.</p>

<p>The order to work in: pick the sauna type your electrical situation can take, then the size, then the brand. Doing it the other way round is how people end up with a quote for a panel upgrade after the crate has arrived.</p>
