How infrared and traditional saunas actually differ

One mechanical difference drives everything else.

A traditional sauna uses a stove — electric or wood-fired — to heat the air in a wood-lined room. You sit in hot air. Pour water over the stones and you get a burst of steam, which Finns call löyly, and the perceived heat spikes.

An infrared sauna has no stove. Carbon or ceramic panels emit radiant energy that is absorbed by your body directly, so the room itself stays far cooler while you still sweat. There are no rocks and no water.

That difference cascades. Radiant heat means lower air temperature, which means less insulation and less power, which often means a standard outlet, which means a lower installed cost and a cabin that can go in a spare bedroom. Air heat means a hotter room, more power, a dedicated circuit, and usually a bigger footprint.

A note on "dry sauna". People use dry sauna to mean both things, which is why this comparison is confusing to search for. Strictly, a dry sauna is a traditional sauna run at low humidity. Infrared cabins are also dry — but they are dry because there is no water source at all, not because the humidity is being managed.

Wiring: the cost nobody quotes up front

This is the single most expensive surprise in the category, and it is the one most listings leave out.

A 120V plug-and-play cabin goes where there is an outlet. A 240V unit needs a dedicated circuit run from your panel by a licensed electrician — a real cost, quoted per job, that no product page includes.

IR sauna vs traditional sauna: the EMF question

Infrared cabins have electric heating panels inches from where you sit, so buyers ask about electromagnetic field exposure. Traditional saunas have a stove, not panels, so the question barely arises.

Here is the thing almost no one tells you: a milligauss figure is close to meaningless without the distance it was measured at.

Magnetic field strength falls off as you move away from its source. So a reading taken 6 to 8 inches from a panel will be lower than one taken 2 to 3 inches from the same panel. Two cabins can both advertise "under 3 mG" and not be comparable products — the one measured at 2 to 3 inches cleared a materially harder bar. A brand can soften its headline number simply by measuring further away.

Dry sauna vs infrared sauna: heat, and how long you wait for it

Infrared runs cooler. Cabins in our catalogue that publish an operating range commonly list roughly 118–132°F with a maximum near 140°F. A traditional room runs far hotter — one published Finnish study exposed participants to a sauna at 98.2°C, about 209°F, at 10% humidity.

Whether cooler is worse is personal. Lower air temperature is why people sit in an infrared cabin for 30–45 minutes rather than 15, and it is also the most common reason someone finds an infrared cabin underwhelming after using a gym sauna.

On heat-up time, we have to be blunt about what we do not know:

Only 39 of our 481 active products publish a heat-up or preheat time — 8%. A temperature in °F appears on 82 of 481 — 17%. That is a category-wide documentation gap, not an InHouse one, and it means most heat-up claims you read anywhere are unverified. Where a figure does exist we will quote it: the Finnmark FD-3 full spectrum infrared sauna states it reaches 170°F in under 60 minutes on a 240V outlet.

As a rule of thumb the mechanism favours infrared — there is no room of air to bring up to temperature — but we are not going to put a number on it that our own catalogue cannot support.

What the evidence supports, and what it doesn't

The research picture is lopsided, and it is worth knowing which way.

Traditional sauna has the larger evidence base. The most-cited work is the Finnish Kuopio Ischemic Heart Disease cohort. In a prospective study of 2,315 middle-aged Finnish men, higher sauna frequency was associated with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality over a median 20.7 years of follow-up (Laukkanen et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015). A related analysis of 1,621 men found lower incident hypertension at the highest sauna frequency — a fully adjusted hazard ratio of 0.53, 95% CI 0.28–0.98, for 4–7 sessions a week against once weekly (Zaccardi et al., American Journal of Hypertension, 2017).

Two limits on that, stated plainly. These are observational cohorts of middle-aged Finnish men in a sauna-bathing culture — they show association, not cause, and they may not generalise to women, to other populations, or to infrared. And in the same hypertension analysis, the 2–3 sessions per week figure was 0.83 with a confidence interval of 0.59–1.18, an interval that crosses 1.0 and does not establish a difference.

Infrared has a smaller literature. A review of published far-infrared sauna research found nine relevant papers and concluded the evidence was limited: moderate support for effects on blood pressure and congestive heart failure, weaker single-study support elsewhere, and consistent evidence against claims about cholesterol (Beever, Canadian Family Physician, 2009). A systematic review of regular dry sauna bathing across 40 clinical studies and 3,855 participants — only 13 of them randomised, most under 40 participants — concluded that the medical evidence for the health benefits commonly claimed "is not well established" (Hussain & Cohen, Evidence-Based Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 2018).

For safety and contraindications, a review in the American Journal of Medicine notes that sauna bathing is well tolerated by most healthy adults, names unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction and severe aortic stenosis among contraindications, and warns that alcohol before a sauna raises the risk of hypotension, arrhythmia and sudden death (Hannuksela & Ellahham, American Journal of Medicine, 2001).

None of this is medical advice, and nothing here says a home sauna treats a condition. If you are pregnant, managing cardiovascular disease, taking medication that affects temperature regulation, or have any heat-sensitive condition, talk to your clinician before buying either type.

Which one should you buy?

Choose infrared if…

  • You have no spare 240V circuit and don't want an electrician in the quote
  • It's going indoors, in a spare room, basement corner or garage
  • Your budget is nearer $2,000–$4,000 than $8,000
  • You want to be in it quickly without waiting on a room to come up to temperature
  • It's mostly one or two people — that's where the range and the pricing are deepest
  • You find very high heat unpleasant and would rather sit longer at a lower temperature

Choose traditional if…

  • You want real Finnish heat and the option to pour water on the stones
  • You already have, or will run, a dedicated 240V circuit
  • It's going outdoors, or you want a barrel or cabin format
  • You care that the larger evidence base is on traditional bathing
  • It's for a household or guests, not one person
  • You've used a gym or spa sauna and that is the experience you're trying to reproduce
If you can't decide, the deciding question is usually the circuit, not the heat. Find out what your panel can give you before you shortlist anything. A 120V cabin you can install this month beats a 240V cabin waiting on an electrician's schedule.

Frequently asked questions

What is the main difference between an infrared and a traditional sauna?
A traditional sauna heats the air around you with a stove, and the hot air heats your skin. An infrared sauna emits radiant energy that warms your body directly at a lower air temperature. That single mechanical difference drives almost every other difference: room temperature, wiring, heat-up time, and cost.

Is an infrared sauna cheaper than a traditional sauna?
In our catalogue as of September 2026, yes. Across 90 active infrared cabins the median price is $3,699, spanning $1,999 to $16,999. Across 57 active traditional saunas the median is $7,999, spanning $4,940 to $49,900. The traditional range is wider because it includes commercial-scale rooms.

Does an infrared sauna need 240V?
Some do and some don't, and the listing often doesn't say. Of 481 active products in our catalogue, only 82 name a 120V requirement and 48 name 240V. 367 — more than three quarters — name no voltage at all. Smaller one- and two-person infrared cabins are commonly 120V plug-and-play. Larger cabins and most traditional electric heaters need a dedicated 240V circuit, which means an electrician.

What does "low EMF" actually mean on a sauna?
It means the manufacturer measured the magnetic field near the heating panels and reported a figure in milligauss. The number is only interpretable if you also know how far from the panel it was measured, because readings taken closer to the source are higher. "Under 3 mG at 6–8 inches" is an easier result to achieve than "under 3 mG at 2–3 inches."

How many saunas actually publish an EMF number?
In our catalogue, 45 of 481 active products publish a genuine milligauss figure. All 45 come from a single manufacturer, Golden Designs Inc, under its own name and its Dynamic Saunas and Maxxus brands. Of those 45, 35 state the distance the reading was taken at and 10 give a number with no distance.

How long does a sauna take to heat up?
Most listings don't say. Only 39 of 481 active products in our catalogue publish a heat-up or preheat time at all. Where a figure is published, one example is the Finnmark FD-3, which states it reaches 170°F in under 60 minutes on a 240V outlet. Treat any unpublished heat-up claim as unverified.

Which sauna type has better evidence behind it?
Traditional Finnish sauna has the larger observational evidence base, led by the Finnish Kuopio cohort studies of middle-aged men. Infrared has a smaller literature. Both are largely observational or small-trial evidence, and neither establishes that a home sauna treats any condition. This is not medical advice.

Is a dry sauna the same as an infrared sauna?
Not quite. "Dry sauna" usually means a traditional sauna run at low humidity, with an electric or wood stove heating the air. Infrared saunas are also dry, but they heat you by radiant energy rather than by air temperature, and they run considerably cooler.

Can you pour water on the rocks in an infrared sauna?
No. Infrared cabins have no stove and no rocks, so there is no löyly. If steam matters to you, you need a traditional sauna with a stove, a steam room, or a combination unit that carries both an infrared array and a traditional heater.

Which should I buy if I only have a standard outlet?
A one- or two-person infrared cabin is the usual answer, because many are built for a 120V plug-and-play connection. Confirm the voltage on the specific product page before buying, since most listings across the category don't state it.

Do infrared saunas get hot enough to feel like a sauna?
They run cooler by design. Infrared cabins in our catalogue that publish an operating range commonly list roughly 118°F to 132°F with a maximum near 140°F. A traditional room runs far hotter. Whether that feels like enough is personal, and it's the single most common reason people find an infrared cabin disappointing.

Which lasts longer, infrared or traditional?
We don't have controlled longitudinal data on either, and we won't guess. What we can say is that a traditional stove is a simpler mechanical object than a carbon panel array, and that vendor lifespan guidance across the category commonly runs 10 to 30 years without being drawn from standardised testing.
