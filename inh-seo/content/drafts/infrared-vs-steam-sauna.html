Infrared vs steam sauna: the comparison is uneven

Almost every article on this question lines up two columns — temperature, humidity, session length — as if you were choosing between two things you buy the same way.

You aren't.

An infrared sauna is a finished product. It ships to your house, it assembles in a room you already have, and a large share of the smaller cabins connect to a standard outlet. When it's together, you're done.

A steam room is an environment. A generator sits outside the room and pipes vapour into a sealed enclosure that has to be fully waterproofed, vapour-sealed to the ceiling, sloped to a drain and fitted with a door that seals. The generator is a product. The room is construction.

That asymmetry is the whole decision, and it gets buried under humidity percentages.

The cost nobody puts on the product page

The generator price is the part you can look up. It's also the part that's least likely to decide your budget.

Our steam generators start at 5kW, and the entry point is a Delta SteamScape 5kW at $2,469.75, rated for up to 100 cubic feet. That's the cheapest way into steam we sell. The full shower packages — ThermaSol and Mr. Steam, with digital controls, shower fittings and aromatherapy — run to $13,985, and their median is $11,625.

What no product page in this category quotes, ours included:

  • The enclosure. A standard tiled shower is not built to hold vapour. Waterproofing behind the tile, a vapour-sealed ceiling, a sloped floor and a door that actually seals are all required, and all priced per job.
  • The electrician. Even our smallest generator at 5kW is well beyond a standard 120V household circuit — 120V × 15A is 1,800W. Every unit needs a dedicated circuit sized to it, and the commercial units at the top of the range need three-phase supply.
  • The plumber. Water in, water out.
  • Ventilation. You are deliberately putting moisture into a sealed room in your house. Where it goes afterwards is a design question, not an afterthought.
We can't give you a number for any of that, and we won't invent one. It depends on your bathroom, your walls, your panel, your local code and your region's labour rates. Get it assessed by a contractor before you buy a generator — not after. The one thing we can tell you is that the equipment price is not the project price, and treating it as one is the single most common way this purchase goes wrong.

What each one feels like

Specs won't tell you which one you'll use three times a week, so here's the honest version.

Infrared is a mild, penetrating heat in dry air. You can breathe easily, read, and stay in for 30–45 minutes. People who've used a gym sauna sometimes find it underwhelming at first, because the air isn't hot in the way they expect — the heat is going into you rather than around you.

Steam is the opposite kind of intensity. At 110–115°F the number sounds mild; saturated air makes it feel far hotter, because sweat can't evaporate. It's enveloping, you can't see across the room, and sessions are shorter. Warm humid air is also a long-standing comfort measure for congestion, and that's the one thing infrared structurally cannot reproduce — an infrared cabin adds no moisture to the air at all.

A traditional sauna with water on the rocks sits between them, and is the reason the combo cabins exist.

What the evidence supports

Worth stating plainly, because both sides of this comparison get oversold.

The largest body of research is on traditional Finnish sauna, not on infrared and not on steam. A prospective cohort of 2,315 middle-aged Finnish men found higher sauna frequency associated with lower cardiovascular and all-cause mortality over a median 20.7 years (Laukkanen et al., JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015). That's an observational cohort of men in a sauna-bathing culture — it shows association rather than cause, and it says nothing about infrared or steam.

On infrared specifically, a review of published far-infrared sauna research found nine relevant papers and judged the evidence limited — moderate support on blood pressure and congestive heart failure, weaker single-study support elsewhere, and consistent evidence against cholesterol claims (Beever, Canadian Family Physician, 2009). Across the wider literature, a systematic review of regular dry sauna bathing covering 40 studies and 3,855 participants — only 13 randomised, most with fewer than 40 participants — concluded that the medical evidence for commonly claimed benefits "is not well established" (Hussain & Cohen, 2018).

On steam rooms we found no comparable body of work, and we're not going to fill that gap with inference from sauna studies. Different heat, different humidity, different exposure.

For safety, a review in the American Journal of Medicine notes sauna bathing is well tolerated by most healthy adults, names unstable angina, recent myocardial infarction and severe aortic stenosis among contraindications, and warns that alcohol beforehand raises the risk of hypotension, arrhythmia and sudden death (Hannuksela & Ellahham, 2001). Heat and humidity together add strain, so a steam room is not a gentler environment just because the thermometer reads lower.

None of this is medical advice, and nothing here says either option treats a condition. If you're pregnant, managing cardiovascular disease, or have a heat-sensitive condition, talk to your clinician first.

Which should you buy?

Infrared, if…

  • You want to be using it this month, not after a build
  • You're renting, or might move — it comes apart and goes with you
  • You don't want a contractor, a plumber or a permit in the conversation
  • Budget is nearer $2,000–$4,000
  • You'd rather sit longer at a lower temperature

Steam, if…

  • You are already renovating a bathroom — this is the only time the economics work
  • The humidity is the specific thing you want, and dry heat won't do
  • You own the home and plan to stay in it
  • You've budgeted the enclosure, not just the generator
  • You're comfortable with ongoing maintenance

A combo cabin, if…

  • You want both heat types and can't justify a renovation
  • You like the idea of pouring water on stones but want infrared most days
  • You want it on a 120V outlet — the FD-4 at $7,795
  • You accept that löyly is not the same as a steam room
The deciding question is almost never the heat. It's whether you're prepared to renovate. If the answer is no, steam is off the table regardless of how much you like it, and the real choice is between an infrared cabin and a combo unit. If you're already tearing out a bathroom, steam becomes a genuinely reasonable addition — and it never will be again at that price.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an infrared sauna and a steam room?
An infrared sauna is dry. Panels emit radiant energy that warms your body directly, with no water involved at all. A steam room is wet: a generator boils water and floods a sealed, waterproofed enclosure with vapour at close to 100% humidity, usually around 110–115°F. One is a cabinet you plug in. The other is a built environment.

Do you sell steam rooms?
Not as a finished enclosure. We checked our own catalogue on 17 September 2026 and not one of our 481 active products is a steam room you step into. What we sell under "steam" is 24 generators, digital controls, steamheads and shower packages, priced from $2,470 to $13,985. Those turn a waterproofed shower into a steam room. The shower itself is a construction job.

Why is a "steam sauna" in your catalogue actually a traditional sauna?
Because that's what most people mean by the phrase. All 15 active products in our Steam Saunas collection are traditional wood cabins with a stove and rocks. You pour water on the rocks and get a burst of steam, which Finns call löyly. It's a dry-heat room with a wet moment in it, not a steam room.

Can you get infrared and steam in one unit?
Yes, in the sense of infrared plus a traditional sauna heater you can pour water on. Two products in our catalogue do it: the Finnmark FD-4 Trinity at $7,795, with 192 diodes and a built-in 1.9kW Harvia Vega heater on a 120V plug-and-play connection, and the Finnmark FD-5 Trinity XL at $8,995, which uses a 4.5kW Harvia KIP heater for higher temperatures. Neither is a steam room and neither has a steam generator.

Which is cheaper, an infrared sauna or a steam room?
Infrared, and the gap is wider than the sticker prices suggest. Our 90 active infrared cabins run $1,999 to $16,999 with a median of $3,699. Our steam equipment runs $2,470 to $13,985, with a median of $11,625 for the shower packages — and that price buys the generator and controls only. Tiling, waterproofing, a vapour-sealed door, drainage and the installer are all on top and are quoted per job.

Is steam better than infrared for congestion?
Warm humid air is a long-standing comfort measure for congestion, and it's the one honest advantage steam has that infrared cannot reproduce, because infrared adds no moisture to the air. We're not going to tell you it treats anything. If you want the humidity specifically, that points you at steam or at a traditional sauna you can pour water on.

What temperature does a steam room run at?
Lower than any sauna — around 110–115°F — because the air is saturated with moisture, and saturated air at higher temperatures becomes unbearable and unsafe. It feels hotter than the number suggests. Infrared cabins in our catalogue that publish a range commonly list 118–132°F with a maximum near 140°F, and a traditional room runs far hotter still.

Does a steam generator need special wiring?
Almost always. The steam generators in our catalogue are rated from 5kW upward, and even the smallest is far beyond what a standard 120V household circuit can supply — 120V × 15A is 1,800W. Every one of them needs a dedicated circuit sized by an electrician, and the larger commercial units need three-phase power.

How much maintenance does a steam room need compared to infrared?
Considerably more. A steam system puts water into a sealed room by design, so it needs ventilation, drying, drainage that works, and generator descaling on a schedule that depends on your water hardness. An infrared cabin is wiped down. If low maintenance matters to you, that difference is larger than any spec on either product page.

Can I put a steam room in an existing bathroom?
Sometimes, and it's a renovation rather than an installation. The enclosure has to be fully waterproofed, vapour-sealed to the ceiling, sloped to drain, and fitted with a door that seals. A standard tiled shower is not built to hold vapour. Get that assessed by a contractor before you buy a generator, not after.

Which should I choose if I want heat therapy without a renovation?
An infrared cabin. It arrives as a product, assembles in a room you already have, and many 1- and 2-person models run on a standard 120V outlet. It's the only option here that doesn't involve a contractor.

Is infrared or steam better for sore muscles after training?
The published evidence doesn't settle it, and anyone claiming it does is overstating what exists. A systematic review of regular dry sauna bathing across 40 studies concluded the medical evidence for commonly claimed benefits is not well established. Choose on what you'll actually use several times a week — which is a practical question rather than a physiological one.
